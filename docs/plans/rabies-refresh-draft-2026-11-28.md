## 1. A cat bite needs the same treatment as a dog bite
> A bite or scratch from a cat can pass on rabies, just as a dog's can. Wash the wound for 15 minutes and go to a government hospital the same day, as for a dog bite. Do not wait to see whether the cat stays well.

Source: WHO, Frequently asked questions about rabies for the general public (post-exposure treatment is needed after a bite from a dog, cat or other animal that is rabid or suspected to be): https://www.who.int/docs/default-source/ntds/rabies/rabies-general-public-faqs.pdf
`[verify]` the "do not wait" line against the national guideline's observation-period wording; WHO's 10-day observation applies only where a doctor decides it.

## 2. Symptoms usually start 2 to 3 months after a bite, but can start within a week
> Rabies symptoms usually appear 2 to 3 months after a bite. They can start as early as a week, or take up to a year. The first signs are often tingling or pain at the bite, fever and tiredness. Once symptoms show, treatment no longer works, so start the injections before.

Source: WHO rabies fact sheet (incubation 2 to 3 months, range one week to one year): https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/rabies
`[verify]` the list of first signs against the same fact sheet before it is written; it was not read in full. One WHO guide says 1 to 3 months; the fact sheet is used here.

## 4. Immunoglobulin (RIG) is given once, with the first dose, for the most serious bites
> For the most serious exposures (category III), the doctor also gives rabies immunoglobulin, injected into and around the wound. It is given once, at the first visit or as soon as possible after it, and is not given after day 7 of the vaccine course. It is free at designated government hospitals in many states, but not every hospital stocks it; ask before you travel.

Source: NCDC, National Guidelines on Rabies Prophylaxis (RIG for all category III exposures; once, at or soon after the first vaccine dose; not indicated beyond day 7). Found via rabiesfreeindia.mohfw.gov.in; edition to confirm (2015, revision discussed in 2019): https://rabiesfreeindia.mohfw.gov.in/resources/uploads/PageContentPdf/174419632223.pdf

## Q1. How soon do rabies symptoms appear after a bite?
Owner's answer: usually 1–3 months, range a few days to more than a year, by bite site and severity; start the vaccine immediately; once symptoms start it is almost always fatal.
**Conflict to settle before this goes in:** the page's own FAQ ("What if it happened days, weeks, or months ago?") says the incubation period is typically 2–3 months, ranging from 1 week to 1 year, which is the WHO rabies fact sheet. "1–3 months" is from a different WHO guide, and "a few days" has no source found. Suggested wording that matches the page and WHO: "Usually 2 to 3 months, but it can be as short as a week or as long as a year, depending on where the bite is and how severe it is. Start the vaccine at once; do not wait for symptoms. Once symptoms start, rabies is almost always fatal." Source: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/rabies `[verify]`

## Q2. How long does the rabies vaccine protect you?
Owner's answer: a full post-exposure course covers that bite; protection fades over time, so a later bite may need booster doses (fewer doses and no RIG if fully vaccinated before); tell the doctor about earlier vaccination.
**Sourced from the WHO 2018 rabies vaccine position paper (owner's pointer, 28 Nov).** What it supports (read from search summaries of the paper and its 2019 journal summary; the paper itself was not opened): people with documented earlier pre-exposure vaccination, or at least two earlier post-exposure vaccine doses, count as "previously immunised"; after a new exposure they get a shorter course and no RIG; and the vaccine is likely to protect for many years, possibly for life, with a booster given at the time of a new exposure. So WHO does **not** say protection "fades"; it says protection is likely long-lasting and a booster is needed after a new bite.
Suggested answer, in WHO's terms: "A full course protects you for years, and probably for life. If you are bitten again, tell the doctor about your earlier injections and show any record: you then need a shorter course and usually no immunoglobulin (RIG). A bite from a possibly rabid animal always needs a doctor's check first." Source: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/who-wer9316 `[verify]` against the paper's wording.
**Conflict risk:** the page cites NCDC 2019 as stricter than WHO on RIG. India's guideline may treat "previously vaccinated" differently (the NCDC text found says no RIG after a completed course, which agrees). Clinician to confirm before this entry goes in. Keep the entry HELD.
